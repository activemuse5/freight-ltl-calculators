Guides

Short, practical explainers on how LTL pricing actually works — each with a worked example you can check against the calculators above.

How freight class works now: the July 2025 density scale

Freight class is the NMFC's way of grouping everything that moves by LTL into pricing bands from class 50 to class 500. The class, together with weight and distance, drives the rate: dense, compact freight earns a low class and a lower price per pound, while light, bulky freight is pushed into high classes because it fills a trailer long before it weighs it down.

On 19 July 2025, NMFC Docket 2025-1 replaced the old 11-tier density scale with a 13-tier scale and moved more than 2,000 items onto density-based classification. The new scale added class 55 (35 to less than 50 pcf) and class 50 (50 pcf or more) at the dense end. Classes that shippers knew for years — 77.5 and 110 among them — no longer exist on the density scale at all. That is why many older online calculators now return classes that no longer exist: they are still reading from the retired 11-tier table, so their answers can be wrong before the shipment even moves.

Density is the headline factor, but it is not the only one. Classification rests on four characteristics: density (weight per cubic foot), handling (how easily the freight can be loaded and moved), stowability (how well it fits with other freight in the trailer) and liability (its value and how likely it is to be damaged or to damage other freight). A very dense item that is fragile, awkward or hazardous can still land in a higher class than its density alone suggests.

Worked example

  1. Measure one piece: 40 × 48 × 40 in, weighing 500 lb.
  2. Cubic feet: 40 × 48 × 40 = 76,800 in³ ÷ 1,728 = 44.44 cu ft.
  3. Density: 500 lb ÷ 44.44 cu ft = 11.25 pcf.
  4. Class: 11.25 pcf falls in the 10 to less than 12 tier → Class 92.5.

One caveat matters more than any other: the density scale is the fallback, not the final word. Your commodity's own NMFC item may assign a fixed class, or a class based on something other than density, and that item governs. Treat every density-based result as an estimate — the carrier's determination is final.

Density in 60 seconds: the one number that sets your class

Density is simply how much weight is packed into each cubic foot of space: pounds divided by cubic feet. It is the one number that sets your class on the density scale, and it takes less than a minute to find. A pallet of bricks and a pallet of pillows can occupy exactly the same space — density is what separates class 50 freight from class 400 freight.

Measure it the way the carrier will. Use the greatest straight-line dimensions of the piece — the longest, widest and tallest points, including anything that sticks out. Measure the whole handling unit: the pallet and all packaging are part of the shipment, so their height and weight count. Then weigh the complete unit, pallet included, and divide.

Worked example

  1. Measure the palletised load: 48 × 40 × 36 in, weighing 800 lb including the pallet.
  2. Cubic feet: 48 × 40 × 36 = 69,120 in³ ÷ 1,728 = 40.00 cu ft.
  3. Density: 800 lb ÷ 40.00 cu ft = 20.00 pcf.
  4. Class: 20.00 pcf falls in the 15 to less than 22.5 tier → Class 70.

Three mistakes that cost money

  • Forgetting the pallet height. A standard pallet adds roughly five to six inches. Leave it out and you overstate density — and invite a reclassification (and a rebill) when the carrier measures the full unit.
  • Rounding dimensions down. Carriers round up, and they measure the longest point, including bulging wrap or an overhanging carton. Rounding down makes your density look better than the carrier will find it.
  • Weighing without the pallet. The scale weight the carrier uses includes the pallet and packaging. Weigh the finished, wrapped unit exactly as it will be handed over.

DIM weight explained: when the box costs more than the weight

A trailer fills up by space as well as by weight, so parcel carriers price big, light boxes by dimensional weight: the box's volume converted into a "weight" using a divisor. If that number is higher than the actual scale weight, you are billed for the DIM weight instead. A large box of foam can weigh 30 lb and still be billed as 38 lb — you pay for the air inside it.

The divisor depends on the service. 139 is the published FedEx divisor and the UPS Daily Rates divisor; 166 is the UPS Retail Rates divisor. The bigger the divisor, the smaller the DIM weight, so the same box bills lighter at a retail counter than on a daily-rate account. Either way, carriers round the DIM weight up to the next whole pound before comparing it with the actual weight.

Worked example

  1. Measure the box: 24 × 18 × 12 in; actual weight 30 lb.
  2. Volume: 24 × 18 × 12 = 5,184 in³.
  3. DIM weight: 5,184 ÷ 139 = 37.29 lb.
  4. Rounded up to the carrier billing rule: 38 lb — greater than the 30 lb actual weight, so 38 lb is billed.

How to fight DIM weight

  • Right-size the box. DIM weight grows with every wasted inch. A box that fits the contents snugly can drop the billed weight by whole pounds.
  • Increase density. Pack tighter, remove void fill you don't need, and split one oversized box into two right-sized ones when the contents allow it.
  • Check both divisors. If you ship at retail rates, run the 166 divisor — the same box may bill several pounds lighter than the 139 result.

Frequently asked questions

What is freight class?

Freight class is the National Motor Freight Classification (NMFC) grouping for LTL freight, running from class 50 to class 500. It is based on four characteristics: density, handling, stowability and liability. Since July 2025, most density-based items are classified using the 13-tier density scale. All else equal, a lower class means a lower rate.

Why did the NMFC density scale change in July 2025?

NMFC Docket 2025-1, effective 19 July 2025, moved more than 2,000 items to density-based classification and expanded the density scale from 11 tiers to 13 tiers, adding class 55 for freight at 35 to less than 50 pcf and class 50 for freight at 50 pcf or more. Older calculators that still use the retired 11-tier scale can return classes that no longer exist.

What is DIM weight, and which divisor should I use?

DIM weight is a volume-based weight: length × width × height in inches, divided by a divisor. A divisor of 139 is the published FedEx divisor and the UPS Daily Rates divisor; 166 is the UPS Retail Rates divisor. Carriers round the result up to the next whole pound and bill the greater of the DIM weight and the actual weight.

What is CWT pricing?

CWT stands for hundredweight, which is 100 pounds. LTL linehaul is commonly quoted as a rate per CWT, so the linehaul cost is (weight in lb ÷ 100) × the rate per CWT. A real freight bill also adds a fuel surcharge, accessorial charges and minimum charges on top of the linehaul.

Is the class from this calculator guaranteed?

No. It is an estimate from the published density scale. Your commodity's own NMFC item may set a different class, and handling, stowability or liability can change it too. The carrier may also reweigh, redimension and reclassify your shipment. The carrier's determination is final.
